\section{Introduction}

The Radio Autoencoder (RADE) sends speech over HF radio channels.  It combines machine learning with classical signal processing to deliver high quality speech (8 \si{kHz} audio bandwidth) in a narrow RF bandwidth, and keeps working at low signal to noise ratios (SNR) where SSB becomes difficult to copy.  The speech quality degrades gradually as the SNR drops, rather than abruptly as with many digital voice systems.

There are two versions of RADE.  RADE V1 was released in 2024, and RADE V2 in 2026.  V2 has a narrower bandwidth, lower latency, better low SNR performance, and a low peak to average power ratio (PAPR) that makes more efficient use of your transmitter's power.  The two versions are different waveforms and cannot decode each other.

RADE is open source.  It is available as a software library that application developers can use, and several applications now support RADE.  This document describes RADE itself rather than any particular application; please refer to your application's documentation for installation and set up details.

This document is aimed at Radio Amateurs, and the regulatory organisations that govern Amateur Radio.  It gives a short introduction to RADE technology, describes V1 and V2, helps you identify RADE signals on the air, and provides tips for operating RADE with an SSB transceiver.  More detailed technical information is available in the RADE V2 test report (FreeDV-060) and the papers listed on the RADE web site.

Figure \ref{fig:radae_block} compares a traditional digital speech over radio system to RADE.
In conventional digital speech systems, the speech encoder extracts features like pitch, voicing, and short term spectrum, and quantises them to a fixed number of bits, e.g. 700 bits/s.  Forward Error Correction (FEC) adds extra bits to protect the encoded speech from bit errors.  The FEC encoded bit stream is then passed to a modulator that generates an analog signal we can send through a radio transmitter over the channel.  The demodulator takes the received signal, and converts it to a stream of bits.  Some of these bits will have errors, which the FEC decoder will attempt to correct.  Finally, the bits are converted back to vocoder features (De-quantised), and speech is synthesised.

RADE takes a novel twist -- the Encoder converts vocoder features directly to Phase Shift Keyed (PSK) symbols.  It effectively combines quantisation, FEC coding, and modulation.  The RADE Decoder converts received PSK symbols back to features that are synthesised using the high quality FARGAN synthesis engine.  The RADE encoder, decoder, and FARGAN synthesiser are built using modern machine learning techniques, and have been trained using many examples of speech and HF channels to produce good quality speech even with the distortion of the HF radio channel.  Classical signal processing converts the PSK symbols to and from an OFDM signal (many narrow carriers sent in parallel), and handles house keeping tasks such as synchronisation.

As shown in Figure \ref{fig:psk_scatter} the PSK symbols from RADE are not discrete constellation points like traditional digital modems, instead they appear to be positioned at random.  This constellation was ``designed" by training.  Interestingly, there are no ``bits" anywhere in the RADE system.  The values from the feature extractor, PSK symbols, through to synthesis are floating point numbers.  The RADE signal can be seen as a form of sampled, analog PSK, built with a combination of machine learning and classical DSP techniques.

\section{RADE V1 and V2}

\textbf{RADE V1} (2024) was the first practical RADE waveform.  It uses 30 OFDM carriers, and has a typical 99\% power bandwidth of 2000 \si{Hz}\footnote{The RADE V1 99\% power bandwidth is set by the transmitter's SSB filter.}.

\textbf{RADE V2} (2026) was developed using lessons learnt from V1.  The machine learning network has been trained to produce a narrow signal (860 \si{Hz} typical 99\% power bandwidth) with a low peak to average power ratio (PAPR), so it makes efficient use of your transmitter's power amplifier.  V2 has lower latency (40 \si{ms}) which gives faster PTT turn around.  It performs 3 \si{dB} better than V1 at low SNRs: 2 \si{dB} from better decoding at the receiver, and 1 \si{dB} from its lower PAPR, which puts more average power into the transmitted signal.

Your transmitter is limited by its peak power.  The Peak to Average Power Ratio (PAPR) sets how much average power you get from those peaks.  SSB speech has a high PAPR (7 to 10 \si{dB} even with a compressor), while RADE V2 is designed for just 3.5 \si{dB}.  Figure \ref{fig:avg_power} shows the result: from the same 100 \si{W} radio, RADE V2 delivers around 54 \si{W} average, two to four times that of compressed SSB, which means a stronger signal at the receiver.

\section{Identifying RADE Signals}

RADE signals sound like modem tones when listened to on SSB.  They are best identified by their shape on a spectrum or waterfall display.  RADE V1 occupies most of an SSB channel, with a fairly flat top.  RADE V2 is much narrower, with a rounded shape 860 \si{Hz} wide, centred about 1500 \si{Hz} from the suppressed carrier.
Figure \ref{fig:waterfall} shows how RADE V1 and V2 appear on a waterfall display.  Unlike SSB speech, the RADE signals are continuous, with no gaps between words.  On a sensitive display the individual RADE V2 carriers, spaced 62.5 \si{Hz} apart, may be visible as fine vertical lines.

\section{Operating Tips}

These tips are based on measurements of RADE V2 through IC-7200 and IC-7300 transceivers, and should apply to most SSB transceivers.

\subsection{Setting Drive for Maximum Power}

\begin{enumerate}
\item Use your radio's data mode (e.g. USB-D), with the audio source set to your computer, and all audio processing off (e.g. speech compressor, transmit equaliser and noise reduction).
\item Set the RF power control to maximum first.  On some radios this control also changes the gain of the transmitter, not just the maximum power, so it needs to be set before the audio level.
\item Increase the audio drive level until the ALC just starts to respond.  Driving harder gives very little extra power: on an IC-7300, driving 6 \si{dB} further into the ALC increased the average power from 54 to 59 \si{W}, less than 0.5 \si{dB}, which is hardly noticeable at the receiver.  However as driving harder does not harm the decoded speech, there is no need to be precise.  On some radios, overdriving may increase out of band emissions.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{What to Expect}

\begin{itemize}
\item With RADE V2, expect an average power of around 45 to 55\% of your radio's rated peak power, e.g. 45 to 55 \si{W} from a 100 \si{W} radio.  This is between SSB speech and constant envelope modes such as RTTY or FT8.  This is two to four times the average power of compressed SSB speech at the same peak power (Figure \ref{fig:avg_power}).
\item Your radio's power meter will fluctuate, and some radio meters respond to the peaks rather than the average.  The power and current indicated by the radio may be higher than the true average.
\item The radio's output power may vary by around 1 \si{dB} during the first second of each over as its gain settles.  This is normal.
\end{itemize}


\subsection{Duty Cycle and Heat}

RADE is a continuous duty mode, like other digital modes.  Your radio will dissipate more heat transmitting RADE than SSB speech, mainly because RADE transmits much more average power.  On an IC-7300 the radio dissipated around 125 \si{W} with RADE V2 at 43 \si{W} average power, compared to 100 \si{W} for compressed SSB at 23 \si{W}, and 139 \si{W} for a full power carrier.  The heat depends mainly on the average power plus a fixed overhead, so reducing the RF power lowers the heat only modestly.  Table \ref{tab:avg_power_modes} compares RADE to modes you may already use.  If your station handles FT8 at 50 \si{W}, it should handle RADE V2.

Antenna components such as baluns, end fed half wave (EFHW) transformers and small tuners are often rated for the low average power of SSB, and may overheat during long digital mode overs.  Check their continuous power ratings.

\subsection{Tuning}

RADE V2 acquires signals within $\pm 32$ \si{Hz} of the correct frequency.  Signals between 32 and 94 \si{Hz} off frequency are detected but not decoded, and the application may display a mistune indication so you can retune.  Tune carefully, and use a radio with an accurate frequency reference.

Just like SSB, RADE will deliver garbled audio if the wrong sideband is selected.  RADE may also occasionally sync on signals more than 94 \si{Hz} off frequency, again giving garbled audio.  If you hear garbled audio, try the opposite sideband, or carefully retune.

\subsection{SNR Display}

RADE V2 provides an SNR estimate (in a 3000 \si{Hz} noise bandwidth).  It is accurate to around 1.5 \si{dB} (RMS) between -5 and +5 \si{dB}, which is the most useful region for judging marginal contacts, and is less accurate on multipath channels.  At high SNRs the displayed value tops out at around 20 \si{dB}, even on very strong signals.

\subsection{Low SNR Operation}

RADE V2 can decode speech at SNRs below 0 \si{dB}, where SSB is very difficult to copy.  As the SNR drops the speech becomes rougher, but usually remains intelligible.  At very low SNRs the receiver may occasionally lose sync and then re-acquire, which takes around 1 second.  Acquisition is faster on strong signals (around 0.2 \si{s}).

\section{Scope and Limitations}

\begin{itemize}
\item RADE V1 and V2 are designed for HF radio channels, which have low SNRs, multipath and fading.  VHF/UHF, satellite and other uses are outside the scope of the design.  If RADE happens to work for you on these channels that's great, however we cannot investigate problems or make changes for them.
\item RADE V1 and V2 are fixed waveforms.  Any change that is not compatible with an existing waveform will be released as a new version.
\item RADE V1 and V2 cannot decode each other; both stations must use the same version.
\end{itemize}
